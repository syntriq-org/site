A decision needs more than an answer

An agreement requires review. A settlement correction is rejected. An operational action is allowed. Each outcome matters, but people also need to understand the reason, the facts used and the applicable policy. A bare yes or no leaves too much work for the reviewer.

Syntriq’s decision architecture separates evaluation machinery from application policies. ChargeWeave supplies domain rules; the platform provides the supported evaluation contract and a consistent way to return the result.

Separate validity from policy

Constraints answer whether the supplied information is structurally and semantically acceptable. Business policies answer what should happen given the relevant facts. A missing currency is different from a valid amount that crosses an approval threshold.

Ontology and supported reasoning establish meaning and relationships. SHACL and domain validators check declared constraints. A versioned evaluator applies business decisions. Keeping these responsibilities clear makes errors easier to diagnose and explanations more useful.

Return the basis of the outcome

A decision contract should return its outcome, calculated values, reason identifiers, missing information and the rules used. Include the relevant rule version and context so a later reviewer can understand what was evaluated.

Consider an agreement with a value above the review threshold. The explanation can identify the amount, the policy condition and the required next step. If the amount is absent, the evaluator should report missing information according to the declared contract instead of silently choosing a business outcome.

Coordinate decisions with action

A workflow can route a review based on the evaluated outcome. A command boundary enforces the permitted transition before applying a change. This matters because an interface can become stale, and a model-generated suggestion is not an authorisation.

Dependencies, conflicting rules and precedence require explicit definitions. The engineering specification must state how applicable policies are selected and what happens when evaluation cannot produce a valid result. Those semantics should be testable with focused scenarios.

Use AI to inspect and improve the process

AI assistance can locate policies, explain their source definitions and propose a candidate change. The workbench then invokes the actual evaluator through the capability broker and returns evidence. That feedback gives the engineer something stronger than a plausible explanation.

A rule change can be compared across routine and boundary cases. The decision trace then connects product intent, executable policy and observed behaviour, making review a discussion about specific consequences.
